01

What the bot does

It is a receptionist, not a salesperson. It knows the trade well enough to be genuinely useful, and it knows where its knowledge stops.

Answers the question

What spray foam is, the difference between the two foams, whether an existing wall can be done without pulling it apart, what happens on the day, where we work. All of it from the five documents.

Takes the enquiry

The same twelve questions the contact form asks, in the form’s own words, gathered as a conversation. Seven of them it needs. The other five it offers, and lets go if the person does not want to answer.

Offers the call

Where someone would rather talk, it offers the fifteen minute phone consult and hands over the booking link instead of pressing for the whole enquiry.

Knows when to stop

Complaints, invoices, warranty questions, anything about work already done. It takes the details, says a person will call, and does not try to resolve it.

The enquiry lands in the same pipeline. Whether it arrives from the form, the phone or this bot, it creates the same contact with the same fields, opens an opportunity at New Enquiry, and every workflow from there runs unchanged. The only difference is the source, which is recorded so we can see how many enquiries the bot brings in.
02

The bot configuration

Three fields under Global Bot Configuration. They govern how the bot behaves. The five documents govern what it knows, and the flow builder governs the order it asks things in.

900 words of the budget. The platform showed 1,790 words left, and the single block this replaces was 1,897, so it never would have fitted. Most of that length was prose making a prompt enforce a sequence: ask in this order, one at a time, never dump the list. A flow enforces all of that structurally, so it comes out of the writing. What is left is the part no flow can enforce, which is what it knows, what it must never say, and when to stop and fetch a person.
The long dash rule lives here, in Personality. It was only in the two AI message descriptions, so a capture step inherited nothing and wrote one into a question about an email address. Anything that governs every message the bot writes belongs in these three fields, not in the description of one action.
Tone of Bot Replies. Friendly is a reasonable pick. If a second one is worth adding, take whichever option means direct or professional. Avoid anything that reads as enthusiastic or casual: this is a trade contractor quoting work on someone's house, and the knowledge base is written straight.
G1 Personality & Style of the bot 190 words
You are the website assistant for Spray It Solutions, a family-owned spray foam insulation contractor based in Carrum Downs, Victoria, working Australia-wide. Write in plain Australian English. Short sentences. Straight and helpful, never pushy. You are a tradesperson's front desk, not a marketer. No hype, no exclamation marks, no emoji. Never use a long dash. Use a comma or a full stop instead. This applies to every message you write, including the questions you ask while taking details. Never say or imply that you are a person. If anyone asks, say you are the website assistant and offer to have one of the family call them. Never give a staff member's name. Answer the question that was asked, then stop. Ask one thing at a time and wait for the answer. Never put a list of what you need on the screen, never number your questions, and never write a bare field name such as "First name" as a line of text. Ask the way someone on the phone would: "What is your name?", not "1. First name". If someone writes to you in another language, answer in that language.
G2 Intent Behind conversation 213 words
You have two jobs. The first is to answer questions about spray foam insulation and about Spray It Solutions. You do that every time. The second is to take an enquiry so the team can quote, and you only do that when the person wants it. Do not ask for a single detail on your first reply. Someone who asks a question has asked a question. Answer it, answer the next one, and let them decide when they are ready. A person who wanted a form would have used the form. Start taking the enquiry only when one of these is true: they ask what it would cost or ask for a quote, they ask to book or to have someone come out, they ask whether we cover their area, or they have described their own building and run out of questions. Offer first, phrased as a question they can decline, and wait for the answer before you ask for anything. If they say no, or simply carry on asking questions, drop it for the rest of the conversation. You can mention the phone number once at the end. Someone who says they are just looking or just researching is not a lead to be worked. Answer them well and let them go.

The flow

The splitter is the intent gate, moved out of the prompt and into something you can see. Everything the bot does before someone asks for a quote happens on one branch, and the enquiry happens on the other.

Five hundred characters is the ceiling on a description field. Every block below is written to fit it, and the count sits next to each one. The three Global Bot Configuration fields are the exception, because that panel budgets words rather than characters. If a field you meet has a tighter limit than 500, tell me which one and I will cut to it rather than you trimming a sentence that is carrying a rule.
A price question does not route. A yes does. Someone asking what it would cost for their place is asking a question with a good answer: we cannot price it without seeing the building, here is what drives it, would you like a call. That answer belongs on General Questions. Only when they say yes, or ask for a call outright, does the trigger fire. Routing on the question itself is what made it ask for a name mid-sentence.
The first reply never offers. The opening answer just answers. Nothing else was forbidding it, and after two sentences about a cold timber floor a bot can fairly argue it understands the building, so the rule has to be flat rather than conditional. The offer lives in Continue conversation, and only after more than a question or two.
The branch is called Phone Call, not Enquiry. The next step after someone gets in touch is the fifteen minute call, not a quote, and the site already offers exactly that. It also changes what the bot is asking a person to agree to. "Can I set up a quick call with one of the family" is a smaller thing to say yes to than "let me take your details", and it is the truer description of what happens next.
Point "No condition met" at the same pair as General Questions. An unclassified message is still a person waiting for an answer. Leaving that branch empty is how a chat goes silent, which you have already seen once.

The second trigger

The general branch does not route anything. It answers, and keeps answering. The second trigger is what moves someone across when they decide they want a quote, by bringing them into the flow again on the enquiry branch.

That gives each piece one job. The splitter reads the first message and decides which kind of conversation this is. The trigger watches the rest of the conversation for the moment that changes. Nothing sits between a person and their next answer.

Describe the trigger condition
The person has asked us to quote, to look at their building, or to call them, or has said yes to being sent the form. That means asking for a quote, asking someone to come out, asking to book or speak to someone, or agreeing when the form is offered. Asking what something costs is not this. Asking whether we cover an area is not this. Describing their building is not this. Those are questions to answer.
It arms only after the main run finishes. The panel says so: a custom scenario fires once the primary workflow has completed, and firing moves the contact to the alternate branch. So the general branch has to actually finish. Continue Conversation needs to be the last thing on it, reaching END, or the run never completes and the trigger never arms. If someone asks for a quote and nothing happens, that is the first thing to check.
Sensitivity: start at Medium, and only ever move down. The two mistakes are not equally expensive. Too sensitive and a person asking a general question gets pulled into the enquiry and asked for their phone number, which is the behaviour you objected to in the first place. Not sensitive enough and someone who wants a quote gets another good answer instead, then asks again more plainly. One of those loses a customer's goodwill, the other costs a message.
Priority only matters once there are two scenarios. Leave it on 8 while this is the only one. When a second is added, most likely a complaint or handover scenario, that one has to win over the enquiry, because someone complaining about a finished job must never be routed into a quote. The panel does not say whether a higher number wins, so set the two and test it rather than assuming.
The warning on that panel does not apply here. It says to capture the details first because a splitter that branches on a stored value needs the value to exist. This one branches on what the person has said, which is already in the conversation. Nothing needs capturing before it.

The other three actions

In flow order. On General Questions the AI message answers the question they opened with, using block F2 above, and Continue conversation runs everything after that. On Enquiry one message hands off to the enquiry workflow after the thirteen capture steps below. Human Handover can fire anywhere.

No readback, and no separate closing message. The acknowledgement email already lists the property type, the areas, the building stage, the timeframe and the postcode, and tells the customer to reply if any of it is wrong. Doing that in the chat first repeats work that is done better a minute later, in writing, where they can keep it and act on it.
Except the email address itself. That is the one field the echo cannot check, because a wrong address means the echo never arrives and nobody finds out. The capture step's validator catches a malformed address, not a plausible typo like gmial.com. So the single hand-off message reads the address back, which costs one line and closes the only gap the email leaves.

Continue conversation governs every exchange after it, for as long as the person keeps asking things, which is most of the conversation. It is also the only place that tells the bot not to capture anything itself, because on this branch capturing is the trigger's job, not the bot's.

The Send the form branch

One AI Message. No capture steps, no field updates, no switches. The form does the capture, and it does it better than a conversation can.

Why the form and not the chat. Every defect of the last week has been field capture: mapping an answer to an exact label, splitting a name, a stale field silently refusing a write. None of it was the bot answering questions, which has worked from the very first transcript. The form rejects a number that is not Australian, enforces four digits on a postcode, makes "which parts" a row of chips rather than a guess at a sentence, and stores the consent wording with its version, its timestamp and the page it was shown on. A model approximating all that will always be the weaker of the two.
The usual objection barely applies here. Sending someone to a form costs you the people who do not go. But the chat window already takes a name and a phone number before the first message, so a lead exists the moment somebody starts typing. The form adds the job detail. It does not create the lead, and nothing is lost if they never fill it in.

Use the staging address until the site goes live. The link above points at the live domain, which is not where the site is yet. Swap it for the staging one while you test, and change it back at go-live, or the bot will send people to a page that does not exist.
One link, not two. The form page already ends with "Would rather talk it through? Book a 15 minute phone call", so someone who wants a time rather than a form finds it there. Offering both in the chat makes a person choose before they know the difference.
Human Handover still belongs here. A complaint, an invoice question or anything about work already done can arrive at any point, including straight after the link goes out. It should be reachable from both branches, not only the general one.
Two records, usually one contact. The chat creates a contact from the name and phone at the gate. The form creates one from what they type. They merge when the phone or the email matches, and they will not when someone gives a different one. Worth checking on the first few, because a split record is a lead the follow-up half misses.

Three things that are easy to get wrong

Store the words, not the codes

Save New build, not new-build. Several of the emails read these fields straight back to the customer, and a confirmation that says "new-build" looks broken.

Which parts takes more than one

On the form it is a row of chips, not a dropdown, and the hint under it reads Choose all that apply. Somebody can say roof and underfloor. Save them as one comma separated list, which is exactly what the form sends.

Company is not on this form

It is asked later, on the scoping call, and only for commercial work. The bot should not go looking for it. If someone volunteers a business name, put it in the enquiry message rather than inventing a question the form does not ask.

The bot never asks about consent

The form does, with an unticked checkbox reading: I agree to receive SMS and email updates from Spray It Solutions about quotes, offers and services. I can opt out at any time by replying STOP or using the unsubscribe link. It stores that wording, its version, the time and the page. Under the Spam Act the evidence is what someone was shown, and a checkbox beats a chatbot reading a sentence aloud. One fewer thing the bot can get wrong.

Replying to the enquiry itself needs no tick. Somebody who asks us to quote their roof has asked us to contact them about their roof. The consent question is only about the marketing that comes later, which is why it is optional and why the enquiry goes through either way.

When it stops answering

A chat that goes silent mid-enquiry is almost never the prompt. The prompt can make it say the wrong thing, but it cannot make it say nothing. Work down this list in order, because it is ordered by how often each one is the answer.

CheckWhat you are looking for
A pause on the contactLook at the contact and at the agent settings for a pause, snooze or auto-pilot-off flag, and for how long it lasts. A seven day pause is a common default. If something suspended the bot on this contact, every later test with the same person fails silently no matter what else you fix. Test with a fresh contact each time.
Where exactly it stoppedNote the last answer the customer gave. If it was the first one that writes to a custom field, which in the asking order is the building type, the field update for that one is the suspect. Standard fields like Postal Code can work fine while a custom field is broken.
Whether the action saved at allThe custom fields refuse to save without at least two output examples. An action abandoned at that error is not there, and the agent has nowhere to put the answer.
Whether the field is a dropdownIf the custom field is a single select rather than free text, it accepts only its own options. Writing anything else fails. Its options have to match the labels in the description exactly, character for character.
What the agent tried to writeAn earlier run produced "Home, 60s weatherboard" where the label is "Home". Free text would swallow that. A dropdown would reject it.
The conversation logThe platform records the action it attempted and the error it got. That turns all of the above from a guess into a fact, so look here first if you can.
Build all nine before testing again. Testing a nine field sequence with three of them built means the run stops at the first gap every time, and each gap looks like a fresh mystery. It is quicker to finish the set than to diagnose it one field at a time.

Four fields the agent should not set

These belong to the workflow that runs when a chat enquiry arrives, not to the conversation. They are fixed values or timestamps, and asking a language model to produce them is asking for drift.

Store the words, not the codes

The website form posts new-build and the workflow turns it into New build. The agent has no such step, so it has to write the readable label straight in. Several emails read these fields back to the customer.

Which parts is a list

The form joins the chosen chips with a comma and a space before sending them. The field is a plain text field, not a multiple choice one, so the agent has to join them the same way or the two sources will not look alike.

Empty beats wrong

Every optional field says so in its own wording. A model asked to fill a field will fill it, so the instruction to leave it empty has to be in the field description, not only in the prompt.

Doc 1 Spray It Solutions: who we are, where we work, how to reach us

Who we are

Doc 2 Insulating homes: roof, walls and underfloor

The three surfaces

Most homes leak heat through the roof, the walls and the floor at the same time. Insulating one and leaving the others helps, but heating and cooling simply takes whichever path is still open. We seal all three.

  • Roof and ceiling. Around a third of the loss in a typical home.
  • Walls. Around a quarter.
  • Underfloor. The rest, and the one almost everybody skips.

These shares are indicative rather than measured for any one house.

Roof and ceiling

Heat rises, and a roof cavity is usually the least protected part of the building, which makes it the biggest single loss in most homes. Foam is applied to the underside of the roof so the house retains heat far longer.

We insulate around roof and ceiling penetrations while maintaining all required clearances around downlights, flues and electrical equipment.

Walls

How it is done depends on whether the house exists yet.

  • During construction. The foam is sprayed between the studs before the lining goes on, filling each cavity edge to edge.
  • An existing home. The cavity is filled from outside through small access points. Nothing internal is disturbed, nobody has to move out, and the access points are carefully made good afterwards.

Retrofitting wall insulation is the job people assume is impossible. It is not. We do it in brick veneer homes that are eighty years old.

Underfloor

A suspended timber floor sits above open, moving air. That is exactly why the floor is cold in July, and why insulating the roof alone never quite fixes the room. It also stops the draft coming up through the boards, and the squeaks with it.

For suitable dry, ventilated subfloors we generally use open-cell LDC-50.

Older homes

Older housing stock is where this pays off hardest. Homes in Victoria built before 1990 usually have no insulation at all in the walls or the subfloor, and many have inadequate insulation in the ceiling.

What actually changes

The first thing owners report is not a number on a bill. It is that the house holds its temperature: one temperature from room to room, warm for hours after the heating goes off, and no draft crossing the floor. Customers describe drafts disappearing rather than merely easing.

The bill follows. Because the heating cycles less often, and runs at a lower setting to reach the same comfort, the saving continues every season for the life of the building.

Noise

Open-cell foam is not sold as a soundproofing product, but its structure does reduce everyday noise. It is soft and flexible, so it absorbs sound energy rather than reflecting it, and because it seals the cavity it also closes the small gaps and air paths that sound travels through. In a standard framed wall with insulation, Icynene LDC-50 has a Sound Transmission Class of around 37. The acoustic benefit is a bonus alongside the thermal and air-sealing performance, not the reason to buy it.

What a home job involves

Every house is different, so the assessment comes first and the detail is confirmed then. What we can say generally: a retrofit does not require the owners to move out, wall work in an existing home is done from outside, and we work around what is already there rather than asking for the building to be stripped.

Doc 4 Spray foam explained: open cell, closed cell and R-value

How spray foam works

Spray polyurethane foam arrives as a liquid. Two components mix at the spray gun, expand within seconds, and cure into a bonded insulation layer. That single property, arriving wet and expanding on contact, is what separates it from every insulation that comes in a cut piece.

Buildings are not built to the tolerances that cut insulation assumes. Studs are not perfectly spaced, cavities are not perfectly square, and services run through the middle of everything. Foam does not care, because it is shaped by the cavity rather than trimmed to fit it.

Why that matters

A building loses heat three ways: conduction, convection and air leakage. Rated insulation values only describe the first one. That is why two buildings insulated to the same number on paper can behave nothing like each other once the wind gets up.

Because foam cures as one continuous layer bonded to the substrate, it deals with all three at once. There are no edges between pieces, no settling over time, and no gap where a service penetrates.

What R-value actually is

R-value is the measure of a material's thermal resistance: how well it resists heat moving through it. The higher the R-value, the slower heat escapes in winter and enters in summer.

It is the number the industry quotes most, but on its own it only describes one third of how an insulation system actually performs. It is measured on a flat, perfect, uninterrupted sample in a sealed piece of test equipment. Nothing in that test involves a stud, a pipe, a downlight, or wind.

Same rating, different building

Two products can carry an identical R-value and behave nothing alike once installed. Take batts and foam at the same rated R-value.

The batts are cut to fit an imperfect cavity, leaving gaps at every edge and every penetration. They compress and settle over time, and air moves freely through the gaps.

The foam expands into the cavity it is sprayed into. There are no edges, so there are no gaps to leak through. It is bonded to the substrate so it does not settle, and it seals and insulates in one layer, closing the air path a rated R-value never described.

The difference is about air movement and installation, not about the number on the bag.

Air permeance

Air permeance is how readily air passes through a building element. Because spray foam is applied wet and expands to fill every gap, join and irregular cavity before it cures, it forms one continuous air barrier rather than a series of separate pieces. That removes the leakage paths around and between traditional batts, which is why customers notice drafts disappearing almost immediately.

Air leakage can account for as much as 40% of the energy cost of heating and cooling a home. That figure comes from the US Department of Energy, Building America Program.

Vapour permeance

Vapour permeance describes how much water vapour can pass through a material. It matters because trapped moisture causes condensation, mould and material decay. Some spray foams are deliberately vapour-permeable so the structure can still breathe, while closed-cell products act as a vapour retarder. We specify the right foam for the substrate and the conditions rather than applying one product everywhere.

Measuring insulation effectiveness properly

Effective insulation is about more than R-value alone. Real-world performance depends on the insulation's R-value, how continuously and completely it is installed, how well it controls unwanted air movement, and how the overall wall or roof system manages moisture and vapour. Even a high R-value product can perform poorly if gaps, voids or air leakage allow heat to bypass the insulation. Insulation should be assessed as part of the complete building envelope, not simply by comparing R-values.

Open cell and closed cell

They are not interchangeable. Anyone who only offers one will tell you theirs is right for everything. The honest answer is that they do different jobs, and picking wrong costs either money or performance. We apply both.

Open cell

Expands dramatically, filling awkward cavities economically. Good thermal performance, though it needs more thickness for the same result. Vapour permeable, so the structure can still dry. Moves with the building envelope without delaminating. The economical choice for large, unrestricted areas. It can also improve the acoustic performance of a wall or ceiling.

Typically used for: most residential roof lines, ceilings, internal and stud walls where drying capacity matters, subfloors, condensation control, and large areas where the budget needs to cover more coverage.

Closed cell

Expands modestly, building density rather than volume. The highest thermal performance per millimetre available, which is what you want where depth is limited. Resists water and vapour, so it suits cold and wet exposure. Rigid, and it measurably stiffens the substrate it bonds to. It costs more, and earns it where the job demands it.

Typically used for: cool rooms, condensation control and warehouse insulation, shipping containers and steel structures, and anywhere thickness is limited but performance is not.

Coatings

Alongside the foams we apply polyurea and aliphatic coatings, and acrylic membrane coatings over foam on roofs. These are protective systems rather than insulation, used where the surface has to survive weather, traffic or a harsh site.

How long it lasts

Spray foam applied correctly will last the lifetime of the building. No sagging, no settling, and nothing to replace as it ages. It is a one-off install that keeps returning value every season it is in the building.

What we will not answer without seeing the building

Which foam, at what thickness, to reach what rating, is a decision made at the assessment. It depends on the substrate, the cavity, the exposure, the ventilation and what the building has to achieve. Anyone who quotes a product and a thickness without seeing the building is guessing.

What the bot must never say

Some of these are commercial, some are legal, and some exist because the claim is not supported by its own source. They are worth reading once before the bot goes live, because every one of them was a real decision on this project.

Never, under any phrasing

  • A price, a range, a rate per square metre or a deposit. The building has to be seen first, and a number in a chat window is a guess.
  • "Forty to fifty percent more efficient than traditional insulation." This was put forward for the site, with a US Department of Energy statement as its evidence. That statement says something different, and narrower: air leakage can account for as much as 40% of the energy cost of heating and cooling a home. Its baseline is a leaky house, not a batt-insulated one, and nothing in it reaches fifty. The supportable claim, in the source's own terms, is in Doc 4.
  • "Customers save 14.5% on electricity." That figure is one customer, one house, subfloor only. Glenn flagged presenting it as a general outcome as technically wrong. It is not in the knowledge base for that reason.
  • Any R-value, thickness, density or temperature rating not written in Doc 4. Under Australian Consumer Law a specific performance claim has to be substantiable on request.
  • A start date, an appointment time, or how long a job will take. The team confirms all timing.
  • Structural, electrical, fire, compliance or building code advice. Those decisions get made on site.
  • That it is a person. If asked, it is the website assistant, and it can have one of the family call.
  • A customer name, a site or a project not already published. SunRice is in Doc 3 because the case study is published. Nothing else is.
When it does not know, the honest answer is the right one. "I do not want to guess at that one. Can I take your number and have someone call you?" is a better outcome than a confident wrong answer, and it also happens to capture the lead.
